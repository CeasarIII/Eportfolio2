Question A

Business Problem That Can Be Solved Using Deep Learning

One business problem that can be addressed using deep learning-based predictive models is customer churn. Customer churn happens when customers stop using a company’s products or services and move to a competitor. This can have a serious impact on a business because losing existing customers reduces revenue and increases the cost of attracting new ones.

Deep learning can help solve this problem by analyzing large amounts of customer data, such as purchase history, browsing behavior, service usage, feedback, and previous interactions. By learning patterns from these records, a predictive model can identify customers who are likely to leave. The company can then respond early by offering personalized promotions, better customer support, loyalty rewards, or other retention strategies.

However, deep learning models are sometimes described as a “black box” because it can be difficult to understand how they produce a prediction. To improve transparency and trust, businesses can use explainable AI techniques that show which factors influenced the result. For example, the system may show that a customer was classified as high-risk because of fewer purchases, repeated complaints, or reduced account activity.

Organizations should also regularly test the accuracy and fairness of the model, compare its predictions with actual outcomes, document how the system was trained, and allow human experts to review important decisions. These strategies can make the model more understandable, reliable, and trustworthy.

Question B

Advanced NLP in Context-Aware Conversational Systems

Modern organizations are using advanced Natural Language Processing models to create intelligent conversational systems such as chatbots, virtual assistants, customer service tools, and educational assistants. Unlike traditional chatbots that depend mainly on fixed responses, modern systems can understand context, user intent, and the meaning behind a conversation.

These systems can dynamically adapt by examining a user’s language, previous conversations, preferences, tone, and communication style. For example, a customer service chatbot may give a short and direct answer to an experienced user, while providing a more detailed explanation to someone who appears confused or unfamiliar with the service.

NLP systems can also use sentiment analysis to detect whether a user is satisfied, frustrated, worried, or confused. The system can then adjust its response, offer additional support, or pass the conversation to a human employee when necessary.

In education, conversational systems can adjust to the learning needs of different students. They may simplify explanations, provide examples, ask follow-up questions, generate quizzes, or recommend learning materials based on a student’s progress. Because the interaction happens in real time, the system can continuously adapt as the learner responds.

These technologies help organizations provide more personalized, accessible, and efficient services. However, they should still be designed carefully to protect user privacy, avoid unfair responses, and provide human assistance when the system cannot understand the situation correctly.

Reflections

Learning insights and personal development

A record of the lessons, challenges, and improvements gained while completing ITC-C508 activities.

WW-P2 Reflection

From Text Preprocessing to Semantic Language Models

Completing the Microsoft Learn module on NLP concepts demonstrated that effective natural language processing begins long before a model makes predictions. Raw text must first be cleaned, tokenized, and normalized. I learned the critical difference between stemming (which chops suffixes using crude rules) and lemmatization (which resolves words to their actual dictionary base using linguistic knowledge).

Furthermore, studying statistical representations like TF-IDF highlighted how term frequency and inverse document frequency identify key terms without understanding context. In contrast, modern semantic models use vector embeddings and transformer architectures to capture contextual relationships in continuous numerical space. This progression from keyword matching to contextual semantic models is the foundation for modern AI applications.

PT-P1 Reflection

Data Balance, Sarcasm, and the Class-Collapse Failure Mode

In the no-code sentiment analyzer exercise using Machine Learning for Kids, I trained a neural network to classify customer feedback into Positive_Praise, Negative_Complaint, and Urgent_Support. Adding 30 targeted supplemental examples improved overall accuracy from 40.0% to 55.0% and average confidence from 68.5% to 80.9%.

However, detailed per-case testing revealed a significant failure mode: all 7 sarcastic test cases were predicted as Negative_Complaint, including cases whose true ground truth was Urgent_Support (e.g., sarcastic urgent delivery mix-ups). Because the supplemental sarcasm data was added only to complaints and not to urgent requests, the model learned the simplest shortcut—collapsing all sarcasm onto complaints. This taught me that rule-based programming cannot handle linguistic subtlety, and training data must be balanced not just by class volume, but across every linguistic pattern within each class.

PT-P2 Reflection

Loss Curve Diagnostics and Hyperparameter Optimization in Keras

Transitioning from a no-code tool to a code-based Keras neural network provided complete control over model architecture, optimization parameters, and regularization. By tracking training and validation loss curves across five experiments, I could directly diagnose underfitting when training epochs were too few (EXP-03) or hidden layer capacity was restricted (EXP-04).

Most importantly, the experiments proved that dataset expansion (EXP-02) had a far greater impact on generalization than architectural modifications alone, achieving 92.3% validation accuracy and a 0.9209 weighted F1-score. Combining embedding layers, global average pooling, and dropout (0.4) with diverse training samples created a well-generalized classifier capable of distinguishing subtle sentiment categories.

PT-M1 Reflection

Learning to Build Reliable Retrieval-Augmented Systems

Building VenomWise helped me understand that developing a RAG chatbot involves much more than connecting a language model to a vector database. During implementation, I encountered several issues, including the deprecation of the originally specified Groq model, changes in LangChain imports, and a metadata-contamination problem that caused the chatbot to refuse questions it could actually answer. I also discovered that repeatedly running the indexing process could accumulate stale chunks in ChromaDB, affecting retrieval quality. Resolving these problems taught me the importance of understanding how each component of a RAG pipeline works and how small implementation errors can affect the entire system.

The hallucination evaluation was another important part of the activity. I tested the chatbot using normal questions, out-of-domain questions, and questions containing false assumptions. The results showed that the chatbot could still reject unsupported information even when the temperature was increased and the exact fallback response was removed. However, I also observed that some responses could slightly overstate the scope of the retrieved information. This taught me that preventing hallucinations is not simply about making a model refuse unsupported questions; it also requires accurate retrieval, proper source handling, and careful control of how information is presented.

WW-M2 Reflection

Turning a Public Health Problem into an AI Research Proposal

Developing the Group ULTI proposal helped me understand the importance of identifying a specific problem before deciding on the technology to use. We initially considered the broader issue of healthcare information accessibility, but narrowed our focus to HIV prevention information, particularly the differences between Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP) and Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP). The proposal highlighted how technical public health guidelines can be difficult for ordinary users to understand, especially when they need time-sensitive information or are concerned about social stigma.

One of the most important considerations in developing our proposal was determining how to make the chatbot informative without allowing it to provide unsupported medical advice. We identified the need for reliable DOH, WHO, and CDC references, evidence-based answers, and questions that would test both factual correctness and the chatbot's ability to recognize requests outside its scope. This activity helped me appreciate that developing an AI application requires more than selecting a model and writing code. It also involves understanding the intended users, defining realistic objectives, establishing evaluation criteria, and considering the possible consequences of incorrect information.

PT-F1 Reflection

Understanding the Effect of Document Segmentation on Retrieval

This activity helped me understand how document chunking affects the quality of information retrieved by a RAG system. Using the Philippine DOH HIV prevention guidelines as the dataset, I compared different chunk sizes and overlap settings through a controlled experiment. I evaluated the configurations using Precision@3, Recall@3, and Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR@3), while also checking whether the generated answers correctly reflected the retrieved evidence. One important observation was that a configuration could retrieve relevant passages without necessarily providing all the information required to generate a complete answer.

Among the tested configurations, C4, which used 500-character chunks with 100-character overlap, achieved the highest overall metric score and was the only configuration to produce three fully correct answers out of three test questions. This showed me that choosing chunking parameters should be based on actual retrieval and answer-quality results rather than simply assuming that larger chunks or greater overlap will always perform better. I also learned the importance of using consistent test questions and supporting evidence when comparing configurations, since the quality of an experiment depends on how fairly and accurately its results are measured.

PT-F2 Reflection

Evaluating Retrieval Performance Beyond Chunk Size

Building on the previous chunking experiment, PT-F2 allowed me to examine how different segmentation settings influence retrieval performance in greater detail. I tested twelve configurations using the FAISS retrieval pipeline and evaluated them through Precision@3, evidence-unit Recall@3, and MRR@3. The results showed that configuration C7, using 1,000-character chunks with zero overlap, achieved the highest observed scores across the three retrieval metrics. It also generated 75 chunks, compared with 88 chunks for the 1,000-character and 200-overlap baseline.

What I found particularly interesting was that increasing overlap did not consistently improve retrieval. Although overlap can preserve information near chunk boundaries, it can also produce similar or redundant passages that compete for limited retrieval positions. The experiment taught me that the best configuration depends on the document structure, the questions being asked, and the number of passages retrieved. I also learned to interpret experimental results carefully because the evaluation used only nine questions and the findings cannot automatically be applied to other datasets. Overall, this activity strengthened my understanding of empirical testing and the importance of supporting technical decisions with measured evidence.

WW-F1 and F2 Reflection

Balancing Model Performance, Response Quality, and Computational Efficiency

This activity helped me understand the relationship between retrieval configuration and computational performance in a RAG application. In the first experiment, I compared different chunk sizes and overlap settings, examining their effects on indexing, retrieval, generation, and overall response time. The second experiment extended this comparison by testing the same pipeline using a CPU and a Google Colab Tesla T4 GPU. The recorded results showed that GPU execution significantly reduced processing time, with average response-time speedups ranging from approximately 23.4 to 27.3 times across the tested configurations.

However, the most important lesson was that faster execution does not necessarily mean better answers. Configuration A was the fastest on the GPU, but it produced a clinically incorrect recommendation for one of the test questions. Configuration B, which used 600-character chunks with 100-character overlap and top-k of three, was tentatively recommended because it retrieved the required evidence for all three questions and achieved the highest correctness and groundedness scores. This activity taught me to consider both computational efficiency and response reliability when evaluating AI systems. In applications involving healthcare information, producing a fast but incorrect answer is not an acceptable trade-off.

ULTI Project Reflection

Developing a Responsible and Evidence-Grounded Healthcare Chatbot

Being part of Group ULTI allowed me to experience what it takes to develop an AI application beyond simply making a model work. My main responsibilities involved building the chatbot application, preparing the evaluation questions and reference answers, running the final experiments, and managing the exported logs and checkpoints. I also contributed to the final IEEE paper, particularly its Results, Discussion, and Limitations and Ethical Considerations sections. Through these responsibilities, I learned that developing an AI system requires not only technical implementation but also careful testing, documentation, and an understanding of the limitations of the technology being used.

One of my major contributions was developing the Gradio chatbot interface connected to our RAG pipeline. I worked on the chat window that allowed users to submit questions and receive generated responses together with their corresponding DOH source pages and response times. This gave me an opportunity to apply my knowledge of Python and application development while understanding how different components of an AI system work together. I realized that a functional chatbot is not simply about displaying an answer. It must also provide relevant supporting information so users can understand where the response came from. Building this interface helped me appreciate the importance of making technical systems accessible and understandable to their intended users.

I was also responsible for preparing the twelve evaluation questions and their corresponding reference answers and key points. These consisted of nine answerable questions and three unanswerable questions, including questions requiring information beyond the uploaded DOH Administrative Order, individual HIV diagnosis, and personalized PEP prescribing. Preparing these questions taught me that evaluating a chatbot requires more than checking whether its responses sound correct. Its answers must be compared against reliable references, and it must recognize when the available information is insufficient. This became particularly important because our project dealt with healthcare information, where unsupported or misleading answers could have serious implications.

Another significant responsibility was running the final chatbot evaluation using Google Colab's Tesla T4 GPU and maintaining the experiment logs, exported evaluation files, and checkpoints. Through this process, I became more familiar with executing experiments, monitoring outputs, organizing results, and keeping records that could be used to verify our findings. I also learned the importance of reproducibility. The results presented in our research paper needed to correspond to the actual outputs generated by our notebook rather than assumptions or manually adjusted values. This experience strengthened my understanding of how experimental documentation supports the credibility of a technical project.

Writing the Results, Discussion, and Limitations and Ethical Considerations sections of our IEEE paper was another important learning experience. Examining the comparison between RAG and the no-RAG baseline helped me understand the value of grounding generated answers in retrieved documents. Our preliminary manual evaluation produced a mean score of 7.583 out of 8 for RAG, compared with 3.167 out of 8 for the no-RAG baseline. However, I also had to acknowledge that the system was not perfect. Our twelve-question evaluation revealed missed evidence and incomplete responses, demonstrating that retrieval augmentation does not completely eliminate errors or guarantee correct answers. Recognizing these limitations taught me to interpret experimental results objectively rather than presenting only the outcomes that made our system look successful.

Most importantly, working on a healthcare-related chatbot made me more aware of the responsibility that comes with developing AI applications. Our system was designed to provide information grounded in a specific Philippine DOH Administrative Order, not to diagnose HIV, prescribe medication, or replace qualified healthcare professionals. Understanding this distinction helped me recognize that technical capability must always be considered alongside ethical responsibility and user safety.

Overall, this project strengthened my skills in Python application development, chatbot integration, evaluation design, experiment execution, technical writing, and research documentation. It also taught me how to take ownership of assigned responsibilities while contributing to a shared team output. Working with Isaiah allowed us to divide the technical and research responsibilities while bringing our individual contributions together into one functioning system. More than simply completing a final project, the experience helped me understand that developing a useful AI application requires collaboration, accountability, evidence-based evaluation, and the willingness to acknowledge and improve its limitations.
